Prototype brief

Scope, and what this deliberately leaves out

Purpose. Test what a narrow, real-feeling Academy lesson actually requires — source material, traceability, simplification limits, and the boundary between what AI can safely draft and what needs a human subject-matter expert.

Scope. The conceptual distinction between Insured, Insurable, and Uninsurable mortgage categories; why the distinction changes how a file should be worked; and when a new agent should stop and verify rather than commit to a classification.

Explicit exclusions. Exact current thresholds, premium rates, lender-specific programs, qualifying-rate mechanics, compliance citations, and any client-facing script — excluded because no Spark Canon source exists yet to state them accurately.

Source limitations. Spark Canon currently has no mortgage domain content. Every specific regulatory figure below is a placeholder. The general category structure itself is a prototype assumption, not a verified source — see the source map at the bottom of this page.

Learning outcomes

Working design tool, not an accepted taxonomy

Know

The names of the three categories and the general factors that distinguish them.

Understand

Why two files with the same down payment percentage can land in different categories.

Judge

Whether a file's known facts support a confident classification, or a specific gap requires verification first.

Perform

Not exercised here — correctly classifying a live file is a practice-and-supervision outcome. Completing this does not establish competence.

Learn

Why the categories exist

Default insurance changes who bears the risk if a borrower defaults, which changes the rate and terms a lender is willing to offer. [PLACEHOLDER — requires Canon-confirmed regulatory framing]

Insured

Default insurance is required and obtained — generally a lower down payment ("high-ratio") on an owner-occupied purchase. The borrower typically pays the premium. [PLACEHOLDER — current threshold, price cap, amortization limit]

Insurable

Down payment is high enough that insurance isn't required for the borrower's sake, but the file still meets insurer eligibility — so a lender can insure it for their own funding purposes. No premium cost to the borrower. [PLACEHOLDER — current eligibility criteria]

Uninsurable

Doesn't meet insurer eligibility regardless of down payment — usually driven by transaction type or property use (e.g. refinances, non-owner-occupied properties), not the down payment amount. [PLACEHOLDER — complete current exclusion list]

Why it matters to an agent

The category affects the rate a lender is likely to offer, whether the client pays a premium, which lenders and programs are realistically available, and what the file needs to demonstrate for the classification to hold up. Getting it wrong isn't a labelling error — it can mean presenting a client with a rate or program that was never actually available to them.

Common new-agent misreading

Down payment is the most visible number on a file, so it's tempting to treat it as the whole answer: "20% down, so it's Insurable." The Apply cases below are built specifically to break that assumption.

Apply

Five fictional cases

No real client information. Numbers are illustrative, not current figures. Work each case before revealing the reasoning.

Case 1

Purchase, owner-occupied single-family, $450,000, 10% down, 25-yr amortization, stable salaried income.
  • Which category does this point toward?
  • What fact drove that answer?
  • What would you still verify before treating it as settled?
  • Would you escalate this file?
Points toward Insured

Down payment is below the low-ratio range on a standard owner-occupied purchase — the pattern that generally drives Insured. This still depends on price and amortization sitting inside current insurer limits, which aren't confirmed here. Strongest answer: "likely Insured, pending price/amortization confirmation," not a flat, fully-settled "Insured."

Case 2

Purchase, owner-occupied condo, $600,000, 25% down, 25-yr amortization, primary residence.
  • Which category does this point toward?
  • What fact drove that answer?
  • What would you still verify before treating it as settled?
  • Would you escalate this file?
Points toward Insurable, not Insured

Down payment is high enough that insurance isn't required for the borrower's sake — but that doesn't mean the file is exempt from every eligibility question, just that the cost falls differently. The common error is concluding "high down payment = no insurance considerations at all," which conflates Insured and Insurable as if only one involves insurance eligibility.

Case 3

Refinance of an owner-occupied home to consolidate debt, ~35% equity, 25-yr amortization requested.
  • Which category does this point toward?
  • What fact drove that answer?
  • What would you still verify before treating it as settled?
  • Would you escalate this file?
Uninsurable — equity is irrelevant here

This is the case most likely to be misjudged by someone anchored on down payment size: 35% equity looks strong, but refinances are generally excluded by transaction type, independent of how much equity exists. Not every factor is a spectrum — some are a hard structural gate.

Case 4

Purchase, non-owner-occupied rental property, $450,000, 25% down, 25-yr amortization.
  • Which category does this point toward?
  • What fact drove that answer?
  • What would you still verify before treating it as settled?
  • Would you escalate this file?
Uninsurable — occupancy, not down payment

25% down looks identical to Case 2's profile, but occupancy (non-owner-occupied) independently disqualifies it. Two cases with the same down payment landing in different categories is the clearest demonstration here that down payment alone is not the whole answer.

Case 5 — escalation case

Purchase, owner-occupied duplex (client occupies one unit, rents the other), price near an uncertain threshold, 15% down, 25-yr amortization, self-employed client with 1.5 years documented income.
  • Which category does this point toward?
  • What fact drove that answer?
  • What would you still verify before treating it as settled?
  • Would you escalate this file?
Cannot classify with confidence

At least three things are unresolved: whether a mixed owner-occupied/rental duplex meets current insurer occupancy rules, whether the price sits under or over a threshold the agent isn't certain of, and how self-employment documentation interacts with qualification (a related but separate question). The defensible answer isn't a guess in either direction — it's naming exactly what's unverified and escalating or checking before telling the client anything definite.

Assessment

Recall, explanation, application, judgment

1 · Recall

Which of the three categories involves the borrower paying a mortgage default insurance premium?

Show expected reasoning
Expected
Insured, by definition.
Common error
Confusing Insured and Insurable, since both involve insurance eligibility.
Why it matters
This is the single most common confusion flagged in the Learn section.

2 · Explanation

Why can two files with the same down payment percentage land in different categories?

Show expected reasoning
Expected
Down payment governs the Insured/Insurable split, but occupancy and transaction type can independently push a file to Uninsurable regardless of down payment.
Acceptable alternatives
Any answer naming at least one non-down-payment factor (occupancy, transaction type).
Common error
Restating that down payment matters without naming what else does.

3 · Scenario application

Purchase, owner-occupied, 5% down, standard single-family, 25-yr amortization. Classify it, and state what you'd still confirm.

Show expected reasoning
Expected
Points toward Insured; price and amortization still need confirming against current limits.
Acceptable alternatives
"Likely Insured, pending confirmation" is strongest; a flat "Insured" with nothing flagged as unconfirmed is weaker but not wrong.
Common error
Treating the classification as fully certain with no remaining unknowns.

4 · Scenario application

Refinance, owner-occupied, strong equity position. Classify it.

Show expected reasoning
Expected
Uninsurable, on transaction type, independent of equity.
Common error
Reasoning from equity/down payment instead of transaction type.
Why it matters
Directly re-tests the single highest-value distinction in this lesson.

5 · Professional judgment

A file shows 22% down. The agent is confident that clears the bar for Insurable and moves forward without checking anything else. What's missing, and would you escalate?

Show expected reasoning
Expected
Down payment alone doesn't confirm eligibility — price cap, amortization, occupancy, and transaction type still need checking before treating the file as settled.
Acceptable alternatives
Unresolved — Spark hasn't yet defined what threshold of doubt requires escalation vs. self-verification. Both "escalate" and "verify independently" are currently defensible.
Why it matters
Same reasoning error as Cases 3 and 4, tested without the scaffolding — checks whether the lesson generalized or was just pattern-matched.

Knowledge check

12 multiple-choice items — Know / Understand only

Supplementary to the Assessment above, not a replacement — multiple choice can't honestly test judgment. A perfect score doesn't establish competence. A couple of items use "verify before classifying" as the correct choice on purpose, so the format doesn't quietly train false confidence.

1

Which category involves the borrower paying a mortgage default insurance premium?
  1. Insured
  2. Insurable
  3. Uninsurable
  4. All three

Answer: A

2

In the Insurable category, who typically decides whether default insurance is obtained?
  1. The client, for their own protection
  2. The lender, for their own funding purposes
  3. The insurer, automatically
  4. Insurance is never obtained on an Insurable file

Answer: B

3

Which factor most directly excludes a purchase from insurer eligibility, regardless of down payment?
  1. The client's credit score
  2. Property occupancy/use and transaction type
  3. The lender's internal volume targets
  4. The client's age

Answer: B

4

Two files both have 25% down. One is Insurable, one Uninsurable. Most likely explanation?
  1. Data entry error — down payment always decides the category
  2. They differ on a structural factor such as occupancy or transaction type
  3. The Insurable file must have a higher credit score
  4. The Uninsurable file must have a shorter amortization

Answer: B

5

Purchase, owner-occupied townhouse, 8% down, 25-yr amortization. Strongest classification?
  1. Uninsurable — 8% is too low
  2. Insurable — the client is a first-time buyer
  3. Likely Insured, pending price/amortization confirmation
  4. Cannot be classified — occupancy is unclear

Answer: C

6

Second home occupied only part of the year (not a rental, not primary residence), 30% down. First step?
  1. Classify Insurable — 30% clears the low-ratio threshold
  2. Classify Uninsurable — it isn't the primary residence
  3. Recognize occupancy is ambiguous; verify before classifying
  4. Classify Insured — always the safest default

Answer: C

7

Refinance to consolidate debt, 40% equity. Classify it.
  1. Insured — large equity cushion
  2. Insurable — 40% is well above any low-ratio threshold
  3. Uninsurable — refinances are generally excluded by transaction type, regardless of equity
  4. Cannot be determined without more information

Answer: C

8

Rental property purchase, 35% down, strong tenant history on other properties. Classify it.
  1. Insurable — 35% is well above the low-ratio threshold
  2. Uninsurable — occupancy independently excludes it regardless of down payment
  3. Insured — rental income supports qualification
  4. Cannot be determined without a credit check

Answer: B

9

"The down payment is 25%, so this file is Insurable." Strongest critique of that reasoning?
  1. 25% isn't actually above the low-ratio threshold
  2. Down payment alone doesn't confirm eligibility — occupancy, transaction type, price, and amortization can independently affect the category
  3. The agent should have said "Insured" instead
  4. There's no flaw — that reasoning is sufficient

Answer: B

10

A file's price is close to a threshold the agent isn't certain is still current. Most defensible next step?
  1. Proceed with the likeliest classification, correct later if needed
  2. Verify the current threshold, or escalate, before treating it as settled
  3. Avoid mentioning the category until every detail is finalized
  4. Default to Uninsurable — the safest assumption under uncertainty

Answer: B

11

Which is the best reason to escalate or verify a file's classification?
  1. The file involves a first-time buyer
  2. A structural factor is unclear, or a threshold is uncertain, and the classification affects what the client is told
  3. The loan amount is large
  4. The client asked an unexpected question

Answer: B

12

Why does the category distinction matter practically, beyond naming it correctly?
  1. It's a required file-management system field
  2. It affects likely pricing, required documentation, and which lenders/programs are realistically available
  3. It determines the agent's commission structure
  4. It has no practical effect beyond terminology

Answer: B

Facilitator / reviewer notes

What this prototype can't establish on its own

  • Discussion beats automated grading forQuestion 5 and Case 5 — both have genuinely defensible alternative answers that depend on judgment Spark hasn't operationalized yet.
  • Requires subject-matter validationEvery bracketed placeholder in Learn; the three-category framing itself; every Apply case number, to confirm it lands where intended against current thresholds.
  • What a supervisor should observe later, in practiceWhether a new agent correctly flags an occupancy or transaction-type override on a live file, rather than anchoring on down payment — and whether they escalate appropriately on genuinely ambiguous files.
  • What this prototype cannot establishThat the learner can classify a real file, that the underlying facts are currently accurate, or that completion constitutes training credit.

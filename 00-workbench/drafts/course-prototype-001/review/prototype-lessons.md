# Course Prototype 001 — Lessons for Building Spark OS

**Status:** Non-authoritative Workbench learning record

**Purpose:** Preserve what the experiment taught without converting the prototype into accepted content

## Outcome

Prototype 001 succeeded as a process experiment even though its mortgage taxonomy failed substantive review.

It exposed real requirements that would have remained abstract if Spark OS had waited to build a perfect Canon or Academy architecture first. It also demonstrated why experimental learner-facing material must remain visibly separated from accepted knowledge and operational use.

## Durable Lessons

### 1. Prototype before full architecture, but label authority clearly

A bounded Workbench prototype can reveal missing sources, roles, review gates, assessment problems, and simplification risks before Spark designs permanent structures.

Prototype status does not make inaccurate patterns harmless. Warnings reduce authority risk but do not prevent memorable false rules from being learned.

### 2. Canon must establish the domain basis before Academy teaches it

Academy may simplify accepted knowledge. It cannot create the domain truth it teaches.

For externally changing mortgage content, a course requires a dated, scoped, reviewed evidence basis before learner-facing use. This does not mean every topic needs a complete Canon architecture before experimentation; it means training cannot outrun its authoritative foundation.

### 3. Correct answers are consequential claims

Fictional case facts are fictional. Answer keys, feedback, rationales, definitions, thresholds, and expected reasoning are derived domain claims.

Future source maps must classify those surfaces separately instead of treating an entire fictional scenario as non-factual content.

### 4. Test simplifications with counterexamples before teaching them

The three-label model compressed several independent dimensions into one apparent taxonomy. It produced clear, memorable, and incorrect universal answers.

Before adopting a taxonomy, ladder, decision tree, or shorthand:

1. identify what it classifies;
2. identify dimensions it hides;
3. test boundary cases and counterexamples;
4. test whether labels are actor- or channel-dependent;
5. determine what information is lost through compression.

### 5. Separate representation from capability

A completed lesson, correct multiple-choice score, signed acknowledgement, documented procedure, or accurate AI output does not establish understanding, judgment, or competence.

Courses may support capability development. Practice, feedback, observation, experience, and human agency remain necessary.

### 6. Separate external facts, practitioner findings, and Spark choices

- Public official sources establish externally researchable rules and product descriptions.
- Licensed practitioners test lender access, overlays, terminology, restricted workflows, and operational reality.
- Sheldon and accepted Spark Governance decide local shorthand, learning scope, authority, and escalation policy.

Do not ask the owner to supply a fact that can be researched. Do not ask a source to decide Spark's commitments.

### 7. Use proportional evidence gates

Gate A supports bounded Workbench correction using public evidence. Gate B protects learner-facing, operational, Canon, or Academy use until practitioner, lender-channel, assessment, approval, and implementation evidence is ready.

This prevents both premature use and analysis paralysis.

### 8. Preserve failed experiments without repairing their history

Prototype 001 should remain as evidence of what was tried and learned. A future successor should be a new artifact with explicit lineage rather than a silent rewrite that erases the failure mode.

### 9. Cross-platform review is useful when roles remain distinct

Independent research, challenge, audit, correction, human approval, implementation, and verification create stronger results when no participant silently changes roles.

Multiple AI responses do not create independent authority. Their value comes from distinct tests, visible disagreements, primary-source verification, and human judgment.

### 10. Infrastructure must earn its place

The prototype revealed possible future needs for source currency, claim traceability, Canon-to-Academy review, and drift detection.

One prototype does not justify schemas, semantic CI, synchronization systems, or a new Governance layer. Record recurring needs and build the smallest mechanism only when repeated use demonstrates value.

## Recommended Future Content Method

1. Select a bounded learning or operational problem.
2. Explore it in the Workbench.
3. Build a small prototype when doing so will expose unknowns.
4. Inventory every material claim, including answer keys and feedback.
5. Verify externally researchable claims with current primary sources.
6. Challenge the domain model with boundaries and counterexamples.
7. Obtain current practitioner review for operational reality.
8. Separate factual findings from Spark organizational decisions.
9. Establish or update the minimum accepted Canon source.
10. Design the learning experience from accepted sources.
11. Validate content, Philosophy alignment, learning design, and operational use.
12. Obtain human approval and coordinated installation.
13. Monitor actual learner and operational evidence before claiming competence or effectiveness.

This extends, rather than replaces, `Explore → Challenge → Audit → Close → Distill`.

## Implications for the Five Philosophy Documents

### Manifesto

The prototype reinforces affirmative humility: Spark OS can support learning and decisions but cannot guarantee understanding, competence, or correct judgment.

### Values

It demonstrates a real cost of representing reality honestly: attractive, completed content must remain unusable when its domain model is materially misleading.

### Principles

It applied the accepted requirements to make the basis of judgment clear, reconsider when evidence warrants, match readiness to consequence, and preserve what future judgment requires.

### Mental Models

It provides meaningful applied evidence for:

- `Representation Is Not Capability`
- `What Changes When We Simplify?`

This evidence should be considered at the next Mental Models checkpoint. It does not automatically make either model canonical.

### Glossary

It shows that terms such as `insured`, `insurable`, `uninsured`, and `uninsurable` cannot be standardized by preference when actors use them for overlapping but different concepts. Glossary adoption must follow domain clarity and identify scope.

## Return to Spark OS Foundations

The course experiment is now parked pending practitioner review. It should not remain the primary project.

Recommended continuation:

1. Conduct a focused Mental Models evidence checkpoint using the prototype as one applied case.
2. Decide whether the two observed candidates are ready to Distill, should remain under observation, or should be reclassified downstream.
3. Resume foundational Glossary exploration using terms already stabilized across the Manifesto, Values, Principles, Governance, and the knowledge/understanding/judgment/competence work.
4. Complete and approve the remaining Philosophy foundation before designing broad Canon or Academy infrastructure.
5. After Philosophy is complete, define only the minimum Canon sourcing and Academy derivation standards demonstrated as necessary by the prototype.

The practitioner review may proceed as a bounded parallel evidence task. It should not block the return to foundational work.
